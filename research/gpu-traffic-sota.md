# GPU traffic simulation: evidence and a Rust/WGSL design target

Research date: 2026-10-31. Target: RX 7900 XT, 20 GB, Rust/wgpu/WGSL, city networks and SUMO-like flows. This is a source review, not a reproduced benchmark. Links identify primary papers, author repositories and documentation actually located/read; unknown means not established by the inspected evidence. No CUDA results below establish AMD/WebGPU performance.

## What counts as a result

Use **UPS = sum over steps of active vehicles / measured wall seconds**. RTF = simulated seconds / wall seconds; with constant occupancy, UPS = occupancy × RTF / step length. Total trips × steps/second is not UPS. Neither allocated agent slots nor completed trips equal simultaneous active vehicles. Report initialization, routing, loading, output, rendering and readback separately, alongside an inclusive elapsed time. Include neighbour maintenance, lane changes, junction decisions, insertion and deletion in the simulation timer. A faster simulator that admits fewer vehicles or drains a queue faster because it ignores conflicts is doing different work.

## Mechanisms worth understanding

### MOSS: temporal coherence, and a version warning

The optimization paper describes snapshot/runtime partitions and prepare/update phases. Its index is a bidirectional position-ordered list per lane, with cached front/back links for both adjacent lanes. Small insertion/deletion/order changes are repaired rather than rebuilding a city-wide sort. IDM and randomized MOBIL are used. Benchmark: 3600 steps on Xeon Platinum 8462Y/RTX 4090. The reported million-vehicle scale does not establish simultaneous occupancy. Road-speed realism is assessed, but this is not evidence of matching SUMO junction throughput or trajectories. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10661v1).

Current code was also read directly: [`lane.cu`](https://github.com/tsinghua-fib-lab/moss/blob/main/src/entity/lane/lane.cu). `Prepare0` removes buffered nodes; `Prepare1` repairs ordering and merges additions; `InitPersonLink` sorts added nodes by position and stable person index; `Prepare2` follows in the preparation sequence. These are lane-parallel kernels with serial list operations within each lane, not one global radix sort. Geometry, lane connectivity, counts and list buffers reside in device-managed structures. Shadow nodes are explicitly checked. Long busy lanes can therefore become serial hotspots; pointer graphs also need translation to integer buffer indices in WGSL.

The [current README](https://github.com/tsinghua-fib-lab/moss) says v1.0 removed junction overlap to avoid deadlocks following CBLab, removed buses, and narrowed scope; v1.1 adds a CUDA memory arena. Its benchmark repository targets older `<1.0.0` versions. Thus current junction behaviour must not be silently attributed to the 2024 benchmark. Current `Lane::IsNoEntry` rejects non-green junction lanes, but that alone is not a complete right-of-way or insertion-conflict algorithm. Exact simultaneous lane-change arbitration was not established in this review.

### LPSim: occupancy grid, not sorted continuous lanes

The [architecture paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08496v1) maps lane length into a char-valued road-status array with approximately metre-scale cells, encoding occupancy/speed. Device vehicle records, intersection state and adjacency lists accompany it. One thread advances one vehicle, scans road conditions, tests departure, junction and lane-change conditions. Multi-GPU partitions use ghost edges, atomic transfer buffers and peer copies; deletion fills holes from the tail. No periodic global lane sort is required by this representation. Mandatory lane-change gap acceptance is represented; this is not SUMO's full behavioural model. Multi-GPU consistency is tested, but not detailed SUMO flow equivalence.

The [published abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0968090X24003942) supersedes the v1 preprint's dual-K80 figures. Its 113× claim is CPU-versus-dual-A100, not necessarily current single-core SUMO. Normalized “CUDA core” comparisons do not transfer to an RX 7900 XT. The ParSim authors separately characterize LPSim signals as simplified flashing-red delay emulation; that secondary characterization should be checked against the chosen LPSim revision before reuse.

### FLAME GPU: lookup/messages; loaded is not inserted

The [2025 study](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-8954/13/5/376) uses agent-state kernels and a global message pool, with network lookups for leader/follower and junction entry. SUMO-generated maps, routes and demand are used. The reported insertion rate differs from SUMO and degrades with population. Teleportation/adaptive lights are omitted, and roundabout and speed-transition improvements remain future work. Its benchmark therefore includes traffic progression and insertion, but does not demonstrate equal admitted work or equal flows. Exact bucket/list rebuild cadence, lane-change conflict arbitration, step length and benchmark GPU were not recovered reliably here. Do not invent them or equate this model with all FLAME GPU examples. [FLAME GPU source](https://github.com/FLAMEGPU/FLAMEGPU2) is a general CUDA framework, not verified source for this particular traffic model.

### ParSim: an instructive counterexample

The [paper](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/13/6960) explicitly compares its model with LPSim: typical 0.5 s steps, floating positions/speeds, fused kernel, traffic distributed over lanes to provide capacity **without lane-changing behaviour**. Junction transfers occur when downstream space is available; crossing-path conflicts are absent. This can overestimate capacity. Bay Area comparison did not operate traffic lights. Bologna SUMO can gridlock, making 1800–5000× speed ratios partly model divergence. Speed/waiting/volume comparisons exist, but no city-wide equivalence follows. Active UPS cannot be inferred from total trips. Arrays are allocated once in GPU memory; avoid treating fused-kernel speed as proof that portable workgroups can globally synchronize inside a WGSL dispatch. Runnable source and precise GPU model were not verified here.

### CPU competitors and the accuracy tradeoff

QarSUMO retains SUMO's local leader/lane/junction processing in separate CPU partitions; its contribution is partition exchange and grouping consecutive slow vehicles in congestion. This retains substantially more SUMO machinery than an IDM rewrite, but border synchronization can change results. The irregular-network scaling deficit is directly relevant to cities. The inspected paper evaluates accuracy against sequential SUMO; no numerical universal error bound is asserted here. [Paper](https://cighao.com/papers/qarsumo.pdf).

CityFlow's paper compares small straight-road trip durations with SUMO; this does not validate junction/right-of-way fidelity. Current [`engine.cpp`](https://github.com/cityflow-project/CityFlow/blob/master/src/engine/engine.cpp) reads `laneChange` with default false, creates thread pools for vehicles/roads/intersections/drivables and uses barriers. Thus CBLab's claim that its CityFlow baseline omits road lane changes must not be generalized to all configurations. Enable and time lane changes explicitly. Inspect the selected revision's lane collections, shadow vehicles and crossing-conflict code before porting; this review did not establish every container/rebuild rule.

CBEngine merges sensing and action computation for locality, supports adjacent-lane safety constraints and mandatory cut-ins, and replaces intersection trajectories/conflicts with holding time. Its speed-distribution comparison with SUMO gives normalized Wasserstein distances 0.0141/0.0124/0.0089 at three sampled times, rather than per-junction flow equivalence. The published synthetic occupancy test gives 100,000 / 0.2610 = 383,142 UPS versus 100,000 / 8.9111 = 11,222 SUMO UPS. Device data: none; CPU code. Exact neighbour container and rebuild cadence remain unverified. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.00896).

GEMSim is a queue model: individual plans do not imply continuous car following, lane-change conflicts or leaders. Its later [ETH thesis](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/bitstream/handle/20.500.11850/601899/saprykin_phd_thesis.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1) separates mobility (90 vs MATSim 9058 s) from total iteration (451 vs 10,232 s, 22.69×), including preparation, scoring, output and learning. These are different experiments from the 2019 68× figure. This is a valuable warning about end-to-end overhead. Public traffic GEMSim implementation was not located; unrelated DNA “GemSIM” projects are not its code.

## SUMO baseline and commercial comparisons

[SUMO's FAQ](https://sumo.sourceforge.net/docs/FAQ.html) defines its verbose UPS and emphasizes junction/lane interactions and inverse step-length cost. Its worked city example uses 80k UPS, not a standardized hardware benchmark. A [SUMO maintainer's 2022 report](https://www.eclipse.org/lists/sumo-user/msg11260.html) gives 50k–300k UPS. The FLAME study's ~116k UPS is a useful measured real-network reference. CBLab demonstrates that a heavily interacting synthetic case can be much lower (~11k). **There is no one single-core “SUMO speed.”** Hardware/version/outputs and active occupancy must accompany a number; no local SUMO run was performed for this research.

Current [options](https://eclipse.dev/sumo/docs/sumo.html) expose `--threads` (default 1) and RNG count. [Routing threads](https://eclipse.dev/sumo/docs/Demand/Automatic_Routing.html) are a separate facility. The [maintainer discussion](https://github.com/eclipse-sumo/sumo/issues/13003) calls simulation multithreading experimental in 2023; that dated statement is not proof of current scaling. An [older parallel-SUMO study](https://sumo.dlr.de/2013/SUMO2013_15-17May%202013_Berlin-Adlershof.pdf) reports four-thread wall time 57–65% of sequential, ~1.54–1.75×; two/eight threads were less effective. Do not claim present SUMO is exclusively single-threaded or linearly scales. Measure `--threads 1,2,4,8` on the actual scenario/release.

A [published Vissim/SUMO/MATSim comparison](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-7590/5/4/201) reports scenario III runtimes 2887/659.9/271.2 s and IV 41,894/16,282/2576 s respectively. This is illustrative, not normalized UPS: queue-based MATSim does different work, and the article's explanation conflates routing and following. No defensible matched Aimsun/Vissim/MATSim active-UPS league table was found. [Aimsun's manual](https://docs.aimsun.com/next/22.0.2/UsersManual/MicrosimulationModellingVehicleMovement.html) documents multiple vehicle models; feature settings matter. Closed source prevents verifying GPU-like neighbour structures. Require identical demand, timestep, junction features and inclusive timers before claiming to beat these products. Vendor “faster than real time” figures alone are insufficient.

## Recent accelerated and differentiable work: relevant, different targets

- **Waymax (2023):** [code/documentation](https://github.com/waymo-research/waymax) uses fixed-shape JAX scene tensors and stateless rollout, with recorded replay or IDM. It provides overlap, offroad, wrong-way, kinematic and log-divergence metrics. Scene initialization from Waymo is not city departure/insertion; general junction compliance depends on policies. No verified active city UPS here.
- **GPUDrive (2024, revised 2025) / Madrona:** [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.01584v3), [code](https://github.com/Emerge-Lab/gpudrive). GPU ECS and task graphs support parallel scenes, observations, collision checks and rewards; dynamics/actions or replay determine movement. This is not a lane-leader model with endogenous city routing and SUMO right of way. Million-FPS language counts agent experience across scenes; policy-training throughput differs. RL experiments use RTX 4080/A100. No SUMO network-flow equivalence established.
- **Parallel differentiable IDM (Dec 2024):** [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.16750v1) studies gradient-based trajectory optimization with an IDM simulator. Differentiable following is useful for calibration; discrete lane/junction arbitration remains a separate problem. Do not assume differentiability of a fixed interaction graph solves changing leaders or city insertion. No comparable full-city UPS established.
- **XMob (2026):** [Google research page](https://research.google/pubs/xmob-accelerated-differentiable-mesoscopic-traffic-simulation-in-jax/) verifies a JAX differentiable **mesoscopic** framework, targeting calibration and parameter estimation. Its claimed orders-of-magnitude acceleration is not a microscopic lane-change benchmark; exact executable source and matched UPS were not established here.
- **UNsim (2026):** [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11380), [code](https://github.com/toruseo/UNsim) implements differentiable Link Transmission Model with dynamic route choice. Link flow conservation/node models, not per-car lane neighbours. Useful differentiable demand calibration, not microscopic SUMO replacement.
- **JaxSim naming:** [actual JaxSim documentation](https://jaxsim.readthedocs.io/stable/) describes robot multibody/locomotion physics. It is not verified as a city traffic simulator.
- **TSLib:** [verified repository](https://github.com/Laysest/tslib) is a 2021 traffic-signal RL framework wrapping SUMO and CityFlow. It is not a recent GPU engine. Likewise LibSignal is an evaluation layer.
- **Kokkos/OpenACC:** targeted searches did not identify a verifiable full SUMO microscopic GPU port with source and city validation. [Kokkos](https://kokkos.org/kokkos-core-wiki/get-started/configuration-guide.html) supports HIP/CUDA/SYCL and experimental OpenACC, but backend existence proves no traffic port. “openACC” traffic search results also include an adaptive-cruise-control dataset, unrelated to compiler directives. Record this as a search gap, not proof no ports exist.
- **Hobby/game work:** [GPU-Traffic-Simulation](https://github.com/ZloyKorovanovich/GPU-Traffic-Simulation) is a real compute-shader project associated with ITMO, advocating GPU-resident agent data. No reproducible throughput, SUMO validation or complete right-of-way contract was established. Treat visual demos as implementation references, not scientific baselines.

## Sorting: measurable compute-shader evidence

| Primary source | Published measurement | Derived throughput / WGSL relevance |
|---|---|---|
| [wgpu_sort](https://github.com/KeKsBoTer/wgpu_sort) | RTX A5000: 1M 32-bit key/value pairs in 317.191 μs; 20M in 3.980834 ms; average of 1000 sorts | ~3.15B / 5.02B pairs/s. R9 380: 1M in 2.76469 ms (~362M/s). Actual wgpu implementation; not RX 7900 XT evidence. |
| [Linebender/Vello sorting notes](https://linebender.org/wiki/gpu/sorting/) | FidelityFX-derived WebGPU experiment ~1B elements/s on M1 Max, large 16M arrays; native Metal experiment ~3B/s | WebGPU/WGSL-relevant evidence; 3B is not the portable path. Notes discuss simulated subgroups and barrier requirements. |
| [AMD FidelityFX ParallelSort](https://gpuopen.com/fidelityfx-parallel-sort/) | Verified algorithm/API/source availability; no numerical throughput established from this page | HLSL SM6 wave operations, Vulkan/DX12, 32-bit key/payload; 4-bit digits, eight rounds, five stages per round. Requires porting, not native WGSL. |
| [wgpu-algorithms 0.1.0](https://docs.rs/crate/wgpu-algorithms/0.1.0) | M3 Max: 100M items resident 273 ms, round trip 407 ms; 1M resident 9.1 ms | ~366M items/s large case, ~110M small case. Includes submission/poll costs; not necessarily same key/value payload as wgpu_sort. |

wgpu_sort's README warns about guessed subgroup/workgroup behaviour; its historical statement that WebGPU lacks subgroups must not be used as a current specification. Query features/limits on the actual adapter and keep a portable fallback. Linebender warns that single-pass lookback can deadlock without forward-progress guarantees and that some apparent sorts are approximate. Stable exact sorting is mandatory when equal-position vehicles require deterministic order. Never assume atomic arrival order is sorted order.

**Budget inference:** at 1B pairs/s a 1M-agent global sort costs ~1 ms; at 84 steps/s it alone consumes ~8.4% of wall time. At 1000 steps/s it consumes the entire budget. Sorting twice, wider keys, lane-range construction and neighbour queries add cost. These are arithmetic estimates, not hardware forecasts. A 32-bit lane-position packed key trades position resolution against lane count; use a stable multiword scheme when it cannot represent both safely. Maintain ID as payload and deterministic tie-breaker. Sort benchmarks must cover duplicate keys and dense stationary queues.

## Five design ideas to copy

1. **Snapshot → propose → arbitrate → commit.** Keep topology and hot vehicle fields in GPU buffers; use integer indices and structure-of-arrays. Read immutable old state, write proposals, resolve conflicts, then commit. Use separate dispatches for global ordering. MOSS supplies the snapshot pattern; arbitration is our added requirement for SUMO-like interactions.
2. **Exploit lane order and temporal coherence.** Start with exact radix sorting of `(lane, position, ID)`, lane ranges and adjacent-lane lower-bound searches; use it as a correctness baseline. Benchmark incremental repair/merge and cached neighbour indices against it. MOSS demonstrates why a full sort every substep may be unnecessary; avoid adopting pointer chasing blindly.
3. **Make junction conflict zones explicit.** Precompute connector conflicts, priorities and downstream storage checks. Resolve competing admissions deterministically, retaining vehicles until safe exit. Use signals as constraints alongside unsignalized priority/gap acceptance. CBLab/ParSim show precisely what speed and fidelity are lost when conflicts become holding time or disappear.
4. **Compact active work and stay resident.** Departure queues, active IDs, lane proposals and completed IDs should use scan/compaction and bounded buffers. Keep routes/topology/state on device; read aggregate diagnostics at coarse intervals. Measure submission/readback separately. GEMSim's total-loop slowdown illustrates the importance of surrounding work.
5. **Build a fidelity/performance ladder.** First match isolated following, merges, lane swaps, insertion, stop lines, protected/unprotected turns and spillback against a pinned SUMO configuration. Then run a real-city network at equal demand and report active UPS, completions, queues, travel-time distributions and detector flows together. Learned/differentiable calibration can help parameters but cannot excuse missing right of way.

## Traps and a defensible advance claim

- Equal totals of scheduled trips can conceal different insertion delays, unfinished vehicles and dropped demand. Include pending departures and unfinished travel time; log conservation counters every step.
- Changing timestep changes both UPS and dynamics. A 1 s result is not comparable with 0.1 s without reporting both. Stop-line overshoot and unsafe lane changes become likely if integration is copied without behavioural constraints.
- Simultaneous proposals can safely fit the same gap in isolation and collide when committed together. Cross-lane swaps and multiple upstream merges require conflict arbitration beyond leader lookup.
- Dense queues, unequal lane lengths and connector transitions break simplistic position keys; a lane change can move the apparent leader backward. Stable IDs and reproducible random draws are necessary for diagnosis.
- Workgroup barriers do not synchronize all workgroups. Do not port CUDA assumptions about warp size, persistent kernels, global spinning or memory pointers into WGSL.
- Twenty GB of VRAM is not a guaranteed maximum storage-buffer binding size. Query adapter limits; chunk large arrays and budget route storage, duplicate state, scratch and outputs. Space per road metre can dominate when roads are mostly empty.
- Matching average speed alone can hide incorrect turning capacity, spillback and queue discharge. Real maps/demand are inputs, not validation. A speedup caused by gridlock disappearing after removing junction conflicts does not establish superior equivalent simulation.

**The number to beat:** use **300,000 active vehicle updates/s** as a conservative published upper reference for ordinary single-core SUMO, and **3,000,000 active UPS end to end** as a proposed minimum 10× engineering target on this RX 7900 XT, with equivalent demand/features and predeclared flow tolerances. This is a project target, not a universal literature frontier. Beating SUMO alone is insufficient for a GPU state-of-the-art claim: FLAME reports roughly 7.2M UPS, and MOSS reports 84.09 complete steps/s at million-vehicle scenario scale. Reproduce a matched workload and occupancy integral before asserting superiority to either. A practical stronger target is **>100 complete steps/s at one million genuinely active vehicles (>100M UPS)** with lane changes, insertion, junction conflicts and spillback included. That number is deliberately a proposed target, not a claimed published MOSS UPS result.

For a “reproduces SUMO flows” claim, predeclare acceptance (suggestion: detector-flow and median trip-time errors ≤5% on held-out periods, plus queue/discharge and tail-time checks), use multiple seeds, and publish failures. Report speed at equal realized occupancy and also equal offered demand. The real advance would be sustained city-scale speed **with a documented fidelity contract on portable WGSL/AMD**, not another very fast unconstrained traffic animation.

### Evidence limits

The papers and repositories above exist; source was directly inspected for MOSS lane preparation and CityFlow engine setup. This was not a full source audit of every engine. Missing numerical hardware/timestep/arbitration details are deliberately marked rather than supplied from memory. No verified 2026 paper located here establishes a full WGSL SUMO-equivalent city simulator. Search did not establish runnable traffic GEMSim, QarSUMO implementation, or a Kokkos/OpenACC SUMO port. Some publisher PDFs were inaccessible; primary mirrors and indexed paper text were used where available. No benchmark was run and no simulator code was modified.
